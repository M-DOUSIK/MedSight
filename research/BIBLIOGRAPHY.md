# The evidence base

Each entry records the claim it supports, the number, and the caveat that
limits it. The handbook is the argument built from them.

`research/notes/` holds a short note for most sources. The three most recent
entries carry their caveat in the entry itself.

The figures are worldwide. Where a source is national, it is used as an
illustration of a pattern rather than as the claim itself, and it is labelled
as such.

WHO 2003 is kept for the baseline it established, not for a number quoted
today. The current figures come from the 2025 and 2026 entries below.

A paper is stored in full only where its licence permits redistribution: CC BY
and relatives, PubMed Central open access, and government and
intergovernmental reports. Everything else is cited by DOI. The licence is
recorded beside each entry, and `research/papers/README.md` lists both sets.

## 1. The problem: medication non-adherence

### [1] Amato et al. 2026 - what non-adherence looks like now

> Amato L et al. *Impact of mHealth on medication adherence in older adults
> with chronic diseases facing treatment burden: a systematic review.*
> **Geriatrics** 2026.
> DOI: <https://doi.org/10.3390/geriatrics11040078>

**Licence:** MDPI, open access (CC BY). **NOT_STORED** - cite and link.

**What it supports:** non-adherence among older patients on long-term
medication runs at **21-55%**. This is the figure the handbook and the site
lead with, in place of the 2003 headline.

**Caveat:** a range across heterogeneous studies and definitions, not a single
measured rate, and it is written as a range everywhere it appears.

### [2] Scotti et al. 2026 - the breadth behind the range

> Scotti L et al. *Enhancing medication adherence in older adults: a systematic
> review of evidence-based strategies.* **J Am Geriatr Soc** 2026.
> DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1111/jgs.70257>

**Licence:** Wiley. **NOT_STORED** - citation and DOI only.

**What it supports:** **128 studies**, searched to December 2024. It is cited
for the scale and the currency of the search rather than for one number, and
it is why the problem can be called current rather than historical.

**Caveat:** a review of strategies, so its primary contribution is about what
helps, not about prevalence. It is not used for a prevalence figure.

### [3] Cardona et al. 2025 - the same picture on dispensing records

> Cardona D et al. *Medication adherence in the elderly population with chronic
> diseases: a factor analysis.* **Frontiers in Pharmacology** 2025.

**Licence:** Frontiers, open access (CC BY). **STORED** at
`research/papers/2025_Cardona_Adherence_ElderlyChronicDisease.pdf`.

**What it supports:** **42,601 dispensing records** for patients over 65. It
matters because it is dispensing data rather than self-report, which is the
usual weakness of adherence measurement.

**Caveat:** a dispensing record shows what was collected, not what was
swallowed. That limitation is the same one this device is honest about.

### [4] WHO 2003 - the baseline the field still quotes

> World Health Organization. *Adherence to Long-Term Therapies: Evidence for
> Action.* Geneva: WHO, 2003. ISBN 92 4 154599 2.
> <https://iris.who.int/handle/10665/42682>
> PAHO mirror (freely downloadable):
> <https://www.paho.org/sites/default/files/WHO-Adherence-Long-Term-Therapies-Eng-2003.pdf>

**Licence:** WHO publication, freely available. Predates WHO's CC licensing
policy, so redistribution terms are not explicit. **NOT_STORED** - cite and
link.

**What it supports:** *"In developed countries, adherence among patients
suffering chronic diseases averages only 50%."* This is the single most-cited
number in the field and the right starting point.

**Caveat, and it matters.** This is a 2003 report and the figure is a synthesis
across conditions, not a measurement of one population. It is a headline, not
a precision instrument. It is used in the handbook to establish that
the problem is large and long-recognised - not to size a market.

### [5] Cutler et al. 2018 - what non-adherence costs, and the spread

> Cutler RL, Fernandez-Llimos F, Frommer M, Benrimoj C, Garcia-Cardenas V.
> *Economic impact of medication non-adherence by disease groups: a systematic
> review.* **BMJ Open** 2018;8(1):e016982.
> DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1136/bmjopen-2017-016982> · PMC5780689

**Licence:** BMJ Open, open access (CC BY-NC). **NOT_STORED** - storable if
someone verifies the file.

**What it supports:** across 79 studies and 14 disease groups, the annual
adjusted disease-specific cost of non-adherence **per person ranged from $949
to $44,190** (2015 US$); costs attributed to all-cause non-adherence ranged
from $5,271 to $52,341.

**Caveat, and this is the reason this source is cited instead of a bigger
headline number.** The review's own conclusion is that the evidence is *"limited
and of varying quality"*, that methods differ enough to make studies
incomparable, and that *"an accurate estimation of true magnitude of the cost
[is] impossible."* Only 10 of 79 studies were rated high quality.
The handbook therefore quotes the **range and the uncertainty**, and
does not quote a single national total. A source that says its own field cannot
yet produce a reliable number is being used honestly only if that is passed on.

## 2. The setting: polypharmacy and error in residential care

### [6] Jokanovic et al. 2015 - how many medications a resident takes

> Jokanovic N, Tan ECK, Dooley MJ, Kirkpatrick CM, Bell JS. *Prevalence and
> factors associated with polypharmacy in long-term care facilities: a
> systematic review.* **J Am Med Dir Assoc** 2015;16(6):535.e1-12.
> DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jamda.2015.03.003>

**Licence:** Elsevier, subscription. **NOT_STORED** - citation and DOI only.

**What it supports, and this is the most important number in this
bibliography.** Across 44 studies, up to **91% of long-term-care residents take
more than 5 medications, 74% more than 9, and 65% more than 10.**

**Why it matters more than any other figure here:** it is the direct measure of
MedSight's single-turntable limitation. A device with one turntable serves one
medication. Two thirds of the population it is designed for take ten or more.
That is not a number to bury - it is the number that positions the product, and
The handbook is built on it.

**Caveat:** prevalence varied widely between studies, facilities, countries and
the definitions used - the review says so directly. "Up to 91%" is an upper
bound across heterogeneous studies, not a central estimate, and it is written
that way wherever it appears.

### [7] Barber et al. 2009 (CHUMS) - the failure this device is positioned against

> Barber ND, Alldred DP, Raynor DK, Dickinson R, Garfield S, Jesson B, Lim R,
> Savage I, Standage C, Buckle P, Carpenter J, Franklin B, Woloshynowych M,
> Zermansky AG. *Care homes' use of medicines study: prevalence, causes and
> potential harm of medication errors in care homes for older people.*
> **Qual Saf Health Care** 2009;18(5):341-6.
> DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1136/qshc.2009.034231> · PMC2762085

**Licence:** BMJ, free full text in PMC; redistribution terms not CC.
**NOT_STORED** - cite and link.

**What it supports:** 256 residents across 55 UK homes, taking a mean of **8.0
medicines**. **178 (69.5%) had one or more medication errors**, mean **1.9
errors per resident**. Mean potential harm (0 = none, 10 = death): prescribing
2.6, monitoring 3.7, administration 2.1, dispensing 2.0.

The causes are as useful as the rates, because they describe a work
environment rather than a personal failing: *high workload, lack of medicines
training, drug round interruptions, inaccurate medicine records, prevalence of
verbal communication, and difficult-to-fill medication administration systems.*

**Caveat:** UK, 2009, and pre-dates most electronic medication administration
record systems now common in care homes. It describes the problem MedSight
addresses; it does not describe today's baseline in a facility that has since
digitised. The handbook says so.

### [8] Chen et al. 2023 - cognitive impairment in the resident population

> Chen P, Cai H, Bai W, Su Z, Tang Y-L, Ungvari GS, Ng CH, Zhang Q, Xiang Y-T.
> *Global prevalence of mild cognitive impairment among older adults living in
> nursing homes: a meta-analysis and systematic review of epidemiological
> surveys.* **Transl Psychiatry** 2023;13(1):88.
> DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1038/s41398-023-02361-1> · PMC10008549

**Licence:** Springer Nature, open access (CC BY). **NOT_STORED** - storable if
someone verifies the file.

**What it supports:** 53 articles, 376,039 participants, 17 countries. Pooled
prevalence of **mild cognitive impairment in nursing-home residents: 21.2%**
(95% CI 18.7-23.6%).

**Caveat, and it is a large one that is quoted rather than hidden.** The
screening instrument used was *significantly associated* with the prevalence
found: studies using the Montreal Cognitive Assessment reported **49.8%**. A
figure that more than doubles depending on how it is measured is a figure that
must be quoted with its method attached.

**What this source does NOT support**, stated because it would be easy to
overreach: this is **mild cognitive impairment**, which is the stage between
normal ageing and dementia. It is *not* a dementia prevalence figure. Estimates
for dementia specifically in residential care are reported considerably higher
in several studies, but no single pooled estimate was found that could be
traced to a source and read. **So no dementia prevalence number is claimed
anywhere.** The handbook describes the population using this MCI figure and
says plainly that the dementia figure was not established.

## 3. The population, worldwide

### [9] WHO 2025 - ageing worldwide

> World Health Organization. *Ageing and health* fact sheet, 1 October 2025.
> <https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/ageing-and-health>

**Licence:** WHO fact sheet, freely published. **NOT_STORED** - it is a web
page, stable at its official URL.

| | |
|---|---|
| By 2030 | **1 in 6 people worldwide** aged 60 or over |
| 60-plus population | **1 billion in 2020** to **1.4 billion in 2030** |
| By 2050 | **2.1 billion**, double the 2020 figure |
| Aged 80 or over | expected to **triple** between 2020 and 2050, to 426 million |

**Caveat:** these are projections from the UN World Population Prospects, not
measurements, and they are averages over very different countries. The
handbook quotes them as projections and as worldwide figures, not as a
statement about any one country.

### [10] WHO - the health workforce shortfall

> World Health Organization, health workforce programme.
> <https://www.who.int/health-topics/health-workforce>

**Licence:** WHO publication, freely available. **NOT_STORED** - cite and link.

A projected shortfall of about **11 million health workers by 2030**,
concentrated in low- and lower-middle-income countries.

**Caveat, and it is the reason this entry has one.** The projection has moved
as the method has: 18 million in 2016, 15 million in 2022, about 10-11 million
for 2030. The fall is not a shortage being solved; the definition and the
model changed too. The handbook quotes the current figure, as a projection and
as approximate.

## 4. What this bibliography does not contain, and why

**No source on cloud-connected dispensers' privacy practices, and no named
product anywhere.** The claim that MedSight's zero-network design is a
meaningful differentiator rests on the *architecture* of the alternatives
rather than on any survey of them: a dispenser with a companion app and an
account necessarily moves the adherence record off the device. **No study of
what any particular product does with patient data was found and read**, so
nothing is claimed about one, and no competing product is named or priced
anywhere in this submission. What is claimed is narrower and follows from the
design: a device with no radio cannot send the record anywhere, and this device
has no radio.

**No source for a market size in currency.** None was found that could be read
and traced, so none is quoted.

**No clinical evidence that automated dispensing improves adherence
outcomes.** This is the largest genuine gap in the evidence base, and it is
stated in the handbook. MedSight has not been validated with any patient, and
the literature on whether devices of this class change outcomes was not
surveyed. **The device is an engineering response to a documented problem, not
an intervention with demonstrated efficacy.**
